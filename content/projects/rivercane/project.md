For the last several years, SECAS has worked to support Tribally led efforts to restore river cane regionwide. River cane _(Arundinaria gigantea)_ is a bamboo native to the Southeast United States that historically formed large stands called canebrakes that could stretch for miles along the floodplain. While individual river cane plants are not rare, canebrakes have declined to less than 2% of their previous extent, which has negatively impacted species, ecosystem services, and Tribal cultural practices.  

Aboveground in a canebrake, dense thickets of river cane stems provide valuable habitat for numerous native birds, reptiles, and pollinators. Belowground, a network of rhizomes stabilizes riverbanks and filters water. River cane is also considered a cultural keystone species for many Southeast Tribes and Indigenous communities due to its significant contribution to cultural identity. Historically, Indigenous people managed and selectively harvested river cane for use in various aspects of daily life, including baskets, tools, fuel and candles, building materials, weaponry, religious practices, food, and more. 

To advance this important work, in 2023, SECAS staff began participating in partnerships like the [Rivercane Restoration Alliance](https://www.spa.usace.army.mil/Missions/TNTCX/Traditional-Ecological-Knowledge/Rivercane-Restoration-Alliance/) and attending events such as the [River Cane Gathering](https://usfs.tribalgis.com/index.php/rivercane-home). SECAS went on to hire three fellows to lay the groundwork for a river cane indicator in the Southeast Conservation Blueprint. After years of conversations and extensive review, [a river cane restoration indicator](https://secas-fws.hub.arcgis.com/maps/fws::river-cane-restoration-southeast-blueprint-indicator/about) became available in version 2025 of the Blueprint! This indicator prioritizes places for river cane restoration and co-stewardship within and near the lands of federally recognized Tribes. As with other Blueprint data, this indicator is a work in progress, but it’s a strong first step toward ensuring the Blueprint represents river cane priority areas. 

In 2024, SECAS also supported an America's Ecosystem Restoration Initiative grant that brought in $3.4 million to help Tribes and partners implement river cane restoration projects across the Southeast. Led by the host of the Rivercane Restoration Alliance, the University of Alabama’s [CONSERVE Research Group](https://conserve-group.org/), [this project](https://conserve-group.org/rivercane) is:
-	Developing a 12-state habitat inventory of rivercane populations,
-	Cultivating river cane seed stock to produce a more robust source of viable plants,
-	Mapping the river cane genome for the first time to ensure the right plants are used in the right places, and 
-	Increasing Tribal Nation capacity to manage and access river cane. 

SECAS staff continue to assist with ongoing efforts to secure funding for river cane restoration in the Southeast!
